One position, two moves: how transposition-table size flips octogen's play

Game seed 500459, octogen vs handwritten, 2 players. octogen's exact endgame solver picks a different opening attack at move 35 depending only on how big its transposition table is. Every finite table from TT8 up to TT18 plays one move; only the near collision‑free TT22 plays another. Both lines still win — but they reveal a real search pathology.

The one‑line mechanism. At move 35 the solver runs a win‑hunt: it exactly solves each candidate attack under a shared node budget and takes the fastest forced win. At small tables, hash collisions evict two heavily‑reused endgame positions; re‑searching them drives one line down to the hard depth 48 recursion cap, which aborts that candidate. At TT13/TT15 every candidate aborts, so the solver returns nothing and a Monte‑Carlo fallback plays 10H,10D. At TT22 the evictions don't happen, KD resolves to a forced win, and the solver plays it.

2  The win‑hunt: why the move flips

The solver solves each candidate attack in order, draining a shared budget of 400,000 search nodes, and keeps the highest‑value resolved win. A candidate that aborts (hits the depth cap or runs the budget out) is discarded. Pick a table size:

JH aborts at every size (its subtree is genuinely deep — structural, not a TT artifact). The story is the rest: at TT13/TT15 all candidates abort; at TT22, KD onward resolve.

5  The decision‑critical evictions

Two endgame positions do all the damage. In a direct‑mapped table each key lands in exactly one slot (key & MASK); a colliding key overwrites it. At TT15 these two positions collide with others and get evicted before they're reused. At TT22 they get their own slots and are reused many times.

K2 is the sharper case: at TT15 it and its collider 16eceb0c… ping‑pong on slot 17545 — each evicts the other — so neither's subtree ever stays cached. At TT22 the same position is stored once and hit 23 times.

6  Why depth‑preferred replacement does not rescue this game

A natural fix is depth‑preferred replacement: on a collision, keep whichever entry sits closer to the root (bigger subtree, costlier to recompute). It fixes some knife‑edge seeds — but not 500459. Two reasons the trace makes concrete:

  • The critical positions K1/K2 are stored deep (plies 27–28), and their colliders arrive at equal or shallower ply (K1's evictor is also ply 27; K2 ping‑pongs with a ply‑25/30 neighbour). A depth rule can't consistently protect a deep entry from an equally‑deep or shallower rival.
  • The thrash isn't one protectable near‑root eviction — it's the pervasive loss of deep transpositions. Every table up to TT18 (256K slots) still aborts the win‑hunt; only the essentially collision‑free TT22 keeps enough of the ~2,800‑position working set resident at once.

So 500459's divergence is fundamental, not a hash‑layout accident: seeing KD as a win requires a table big enough to hold the whole endgame's transposition set without eviction.

Method & what was pruned

Traces were produced by instrumenting sim_solve_rec in c/src/cordite_sim.c behind a compile‑time -DCD_TT_TRACE flag (off in normal builds), dumping every node ENTER/KEY/RET plus every TT STORE, HIT and EVICT for the move‑35 win‑hunt only (group 4), at TT13/TT15/TT22. The KD search visits 55k–166k nodes; this page shows the 10‑candidate win‑hunt summary (complete) and the 48‑ply thrash descent (the exact root→depth‑48 path, complete) — the tens of thousands of sibling subtrees are summarised as counts, not drawn. Node counts in the bar chart are budget nodes spent; the KD table lists total search nodes visited (which additionally counts terminals and TT hits). Values are depth‑relative: v≈984 means "a forced win in a few plies," sign = win/loss for octogen.
