# adventurer: design and measurements

A proof of concept, built 2026-10-21/22 on branch `adventurer`, of wandrer.earth as a second product on c_base.
Every number below was measured on this Mac (Apple M-series, 8 cores, macOS), first against the Rhode Island OpenStreetMap extract and then against New York City and its neighbours, with the machine otherwise quiet unless said.
Wandrer's own stack, from their engineering blog, is Postgres + PostGIS with a 100 GB per-user segments table, tippecanoe-pregenerated per-user MBTiles archives served by nginx, untraveled tiles computed on demand in seconds and cached for five minutes, and processing delays of hours on a weekend.
Adventurer is one C process.

## The shape

One `cbase serve` process (c_base's loop, request pool, HTTP, WebSocket, SQLite store, config and CPU knob, linked by source with no file copied), with adventurer as the linked `cb_app`.
State is authoritative in memory: every user's coverage as sorted `(segment, bike mask, foot mask)` entries, per-user region progress and achievements, per-region monthly and yearly top-3 boards, and one owner word per segment for super-unique.
SQLite is an append-only journal of per-activity results (the hits the matcher found and the numbers the feed showed), written through the platform's group commit and replayed through the same apply at start; no request reads it.
The road graph is one flat file, mmap'd: segments between intersections ordered on a Hilbert curve, int32 first point and int16 shape deltas, intersection nodes with adjacency, a dense z16 cell index, regions from OSM administrative boundaries, a CRC-32C and every index checked at open.
The matcher is an HMM in C: downsample to 15 m, candidates from the cell index within 35 m, Viterbi with arithmetic transitions on the same segment, across a shared node or two hops through one, a bounded Dijkstra for the rest with its answers cached per pair for the match, an off-road state, a CPU budget per activity, one 3.4 MB scratch per thread and no allocation per match.
Tiles are rendered on request straight into MVT protobuf from the graph and the user's coverage: roads, traveled, untraveled (the inverted mask, from z11 up) and unique layers; base tiles are cached per graph; user tiles carry an ETag of the coverage version.
The wire to the web is fixed little-endian structs with a layout hash in every answer; the decoder is generated by shared/tools/structgen from the structs themselves through libclang, and no TypeScript spells a byte offset.
Uploads wait in two byte-bounded lanes, live before backfill (c_base's `cb_lanes`, lifted out of this product), and matcher workers apply results under a striped per-user lock; tiles render inline on the loop thread with a try-lock, so a merge in progress sends the tile to the pool instead of stalling the loop.
The live feed is c_base realtime: one anonymous topic family whose one room is "feed"; the page opens the platform's `GET /ws/public`, says an empty HELLO and SUBs the room, and the hub delivers each push exactly as it delivers foolish's spectators' casts (PR #429 proves that path's machine code unchanged).
A fake world (adv-gen) stands in for Strava: deterministic users of five kinds riding, running and walking the real roads with AR(1) GPS error, dropouts, spikes and a hostile upload every fiftieth; each upload carries the true coverage so the server scores its own matcher.
Where they live and where they go is read from the map (below, "The fake world follows the people").

## The fake world follows the people

A home used to be the first point of a uniformly random segment, which put most homes in suburbs of many short cul-de-sacs (Staten Island, Bergen, Nassau) and few in Manhattan.
Now the importer keeps each boundary's `population=` tag, and a home is drawn with probability proportional to the length of a street people live on (residential, living, tertiary, unclassified, secondary) times its region's density: the region's own people over its length of such streets.
So a region's share of homes is its share of the people, and inside it homes follow the streets.
A town's tag counts the villages inside it, whose streets are the villages', so a region's own people are its tag less the tags of the regions nested in it (nesting judged by the boxes the file keeps); an untagged region gets the mean density.
Urbanity (0 at 500 people per km of street lived on, 1 at 2,000, log-linear) sets the kinds of user (the city has more runners and walkers, the suburbs more road riders), the distances (a city bike commute is 6-14 km, a suburban one 14-30), and how often an activity starts at an attractor instead of the door: a path, footway, pedestrian street or cycleway within 5 km for a run or walk (0.2 + 0.5 x urbanity), a cycleway or signed cycle route (`route=bicycle` members, flagged by the importer) within 12 km for an explorer's ride (0.3), drawn by length, so Central Park, Prospect Park and the Hudson River Greenway pull hardest without being named anywhere.
Routes favour those roads three to one and take primary roads and trunks at 0.4.

Homes of 600 users against the 2020 census shares of the 13.3 M: Brooklyn 22.0% (20.6), Queens 21.3% (18.1), the Bronx 12.2% (11.1), Manhattan 11.3% (12.7), Hempstead 5.0% (6.0), Staten Island 3.3% (3.7), Jersey City 2.5% (2.2).
After 38,000 simulated activities the most-travelled roads are Central Park's East, Center and West Drives (60 users each), then Prospect Park's drives.
The generator runs far faster than life (eight uploads a second is a city's year in days), so the page shows "Simulated time": the uploads so far as months of these users riding three times a week.

What changed, each found by `sample` on the server under that load first: every layer is one MULTILINESTRING per tag tuple instead of a feature per line (feature headers and ids were most of a tile's bytes and of its encoding time, 37% of the busy samples); below the shape zoom a segment is drawn as its two nodes through a per-tile Mercator series, so its shape is not decoded and no tan or log is spent on it (shape decoding and projection were 25%); Everyone's tiles are kept and served for up to 5 s past their version, so a tile renders at most once per window whatever the viewers; the cell walk and the matcher's gather test a sequential run of boxes before touching a segment record (3%).
A differential test renders every tile of a grid from z4 to z16 both ways and requires the same lines under the same tags.

## World scale

The planet, as the question was put: 76 M km of road, 100,000 users, 10 M activities a year.
Each line below is a measured per-unit cost times the planet's units; where the two graphs disagree the dearer one is used.

- **Graph.** The planet is mostly rural, so Rhode Island's 9.3 segments a km is the better guide than New York's 13.4: about 710 M segments. At 93-101 bytes a segment that is a 66-72 GB file. That is 44 times the 16 M segment ids the file format allows today (ADV_MAX_SEGS, 24 bits), so the ids must go to a full u32 (4 G), and no single small VM holds it. Most rides touch one metro area, so the file must be cut by region (a graph per continent or country, users routed to theirs) or mmap'd from a box whose page cache holds the hot areas.
- **Per-segment arrays.** The server keeps several arrays sized to the graph: Everyone's composite (12 B a segment, 8.5 GB), the owners for super-unique (4 B, 2.8 GB), the cell boxes built at open (16 B per cell entry, about 1.2 entries a segment, 14 GB) and a tile thread's visited stamps (4 B a segment, 2.8 GB per tile thread). These, not the coverage, are what break first.
- **Coverage RAM.** 8 bytes a covered segment. New York's simulated users hold 273 km each after 63 activities (3,660 segments); a year of 100 activities in a mostly rural world is perhaps 5,000-8,000 segments, 40-64 KB a user. 100,000 users: 4-6 GB, growing with the years.
- **CPU per activity.** 1.35 ms matched plus a fraction for the merge. 10 M a year is 0.32 a second on average; a peak of twenty times that is 6.4 a second, about 1% of one core. Onboarding is the real load: 100,000 users bringing 500 old activities each is 50 M activities, about 19 core-hours, which the backfill lane already keeps behind live uploads.
- **Tiles.** A tile's cost depends on its density, not on the size of the graph, so the per-tile numbers above hold anywhere: user tiles 0.6 ms p50, a dense city's Everyone low zoom 6 ms to render, and once per 5 s at most. Everyone below about z9 is the exception: a z6 tile of Europe would walk millions of segments. It needs a pre-built low-zoom pyramid, updated as coverage changes rather than rendered on request.
- **Restart.** Replaying the journal costs 23 us an activity, so a year of the planet (10 M) is 3.8 minutes and five years 19 minutes, plus reading a journal of 1.6 KB an activity (16 GB a year).

The three things that would have to get faster first:

1. **Restart: from minutes to seconds.** Snapshot coverage, the composite, the boards and the owners as flat files every N minutes, so a restart maps them and replays only the journal's tail. This takes five years of history from 19 minutes to the tail's seconds.
2. **Memory per segment: from about 40 bytes on every segment of the planet to bytes only where someone has been.** Store the composite and owners only for covered segments (a hash keyed by segment, or per-region arrays loaded when a region is first touched), keep tile stamps per tile instead of per graph, and build cell boxes per region. At 710 M segments the current layout needs about 28 GB with one tile thread before a single user exists.
3. **Graph size and ids: from 72 GB in one file with 24-bit ids to regional files with 32-bit ids.** Then each region's graph fits the RAM of the box that serves it (New York's is 51 MB, all of the United States perhaps 15 GB).

The matcher is not on that list: at 1.35 ms an activity the planet's yearly volume is under four hours of one core.

## Where the time goes

`sample` over a no-inline build of adv-bench (the matcher on Rhode Island), before and after the tuning pass:

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| gather (the candidate walk over the cells, bbox tests, dedupe) | 37% | 27% |
| local search (Dijkstra, heap, node table) | 33% | 24% |
| the Viterbi loop itself | 16% | 26% |
| projection onto shapes (point-segment distance, delta decode) | 8% | 13% |
| mercator (log, cos) for the cell of each sample | 4% | 4% |

The pass: a neighbour cell is walked only when the 35 m radius reaches into it (a cell is 600 m); a transition that skips one short segment at a crossing is priced by arithmetic (two hops through a shared node); a local search's answer for a pair of segments is cached for the rest of the match, since candidates persist for many samples at 15 m spacing.
Result: mean 2718 to 1563 us per ride, p99 14.5 to 7.0 ms, searches 2.33 M to 0.48 M per 1000 rides, recall and precision unchanged to the third decimal.
Finer cells (z17) were tried and reverted: 2% faster, a little less precise.
The second pass came from the bench's three worst rides, all runs matched 70% onto the footway beside the street: OSM draws a sidewalk (`footway=sidewalk`) and every crossing as ways of their own a few metres from the street, sharing its nodes at every corner, so the matcher had two equally good roads under every runner.
The importer skips them (23% of the segments), which took the mean to 1.21 ms, recall to 0.989 and precision to 0.991; a cost of 0.3 to leave a segment (nothing to stay) bought another 0.2% of precision at no recall.
What is left: the cell walk is bbox tests over a few hundred listed segments per sample in a city cell, and a quarter of the searches still fail (parallel paths not joined within the cutoff) at the full expansion cap.

Inside the server the matcher is 90% of the CPU under a burst; the loop thread's own work (HTTP, the feed, the tiles) is a few percent, and the journal's group commit a few more.

## Real, simplified, and missing

Real: the OSM extract and its legal access rules, the graph, the matcher, coverage and region scoring, achievements, monthly and yearly boards, super-unique, the tiles, the journal and recovery, the feed, the site.
Simplified for the proof of concept: no accounts or Strava (the roster is posted by the generator and uploads carry a user id), one region file, the boards keyed by the activity's start month, super-unique at segment rather than sixteenth granularity, the accuracy metric from a truth trailer only a fake upload has.
Missing against Wandrer: no Garmin or Strava connector, no pretty basemap (our own grey roads over a paper background), no history of activities per user in the UI, no bonus points for achievements (the thresholds light up, no score is added), no per-user coverage of more than one region file.

## Known gaps

- A quarter of the local searches on real roads fail at the expansion cap: parallel footways not joined within 3 times the sample spacing plus 200 m; each costs about 30 expansions.
- The route cache's stored distance is covered only by adv-bench's recall staying put; the unit tests pass with it zeroed, because on a grid every transition is arithmetic.
- The worst ride in the bench matches 82% of its truth, a run on paths with parallels the sidewalk rule does not cover.
- A rejected upload's activity id is reused after a restart (it was never journaled).
- z11 and z12 base tiles of a city core were 300-650 KB; grouped into one feature per class they are a quarter of that, and a client cache (the ETag is the graph's CRC) makes them a one-time cost.
- A low-zoom base tile (z8: every cell of the grid, majors only) is rendered once per graph and served from the cache after; the first visitor pays about 10 ms for it.
- `next dev` writes an AGENTS.md into web/ (gitignored); its floating dev indicator is turned off in next.config.ts; the production build is untested.
- The worst bench ride on New York matches 69% of a 24 m truth (a stub), and New York's points cost a third more than Rhode Island's: more two-hop jumps and failed searches in dense grids, not yet profiled down to the instruction.
- A tile below the shape zoom places a partial run of a segment on the chord between its nodes, not on its shape: the drawn line is that chord anyway.
- The Everyone tile cache can serve a tile up to 5 s older than the composite, so neighbouring tiles may differ by a few uploads for that long.
- The composite and the owners are arrays over every segment and the tile stamps are per thread per segment: fine for a metro area, the first thing to change for a country (World scale above).
- Super-unique is per segment: a sixteenth of a segment another user touched makes the whole segment shared.
